# Open-core explorEDA, but earn adoption before charging

**Recommendation.** Sell explorEDA as a commercial component, but structure it now and charge later. Adopt the AG Grid/MUI X open-core model today: relicense the existing `exploreda` package under MIT as the free core, and reserve a commercially licensed `exploreda-pro` superset (project mode, worker execution, row tracing, advanced chart families, embedding slots, DSL CLI) under a proprietary EULA enforced by an offline key and a watermark. Do not try to invoice anyone in the next 90 days: the repo has no license at any level, npm `0.1.0` trails the demo by 112 unreleased changesets, there is no stability promise, no support channel, and 0 stars, so every automated and human procurement check fails today ([readiness audit](notes/exploreda_readiness_and_feature_split.md)). Every solo founder with public numbers spent roughly three years in free distribution before paid revenue mattered: fullPage.js was free for three years before its first $1k month ([Indie Hustle](https://indiehustle.beehiiv.com/p/this-open-source-component-makes-12-000-every-month)), Pintura took three years to reach $5k/month ([Plugged In](https://matthews-newsletter-35a04a.beehiiv.com/p/rik-schennink-grew-pintura-50kmonth-focusing)), and SciChart's founder quit his job 2.5 years after the first sale ([Mixergy](https://mixergy.com/interviews/scichart-with-andrew-burnett-thompson/)). The first paid tier should be **Pro at $299 per developer per year** (annual, perpetual production rights to versions released during the term, 30-day trial key, no seat minimum), anchored to MUI X Pro's $299 ([MUI pricing](https://mui.com/pricing/)) and below AG Grid's $999 perpetual ([AG Grid pricing](https://www.ag-grid.com/license-pricing/)), with an **OEM/SaaS-embedding add-on at $990 per product per year** modeled on fullPage.js ([alvarotrigo.com](https://alvarotrigo.com/fullPage/pricing/)). Decide the license split before anyone adopts the package, because the Handsontable, HashiCorp and Fluent Assertions episodes show relicensing after adoption is what triggers forks and backlash, and with no outside contributors there is zero backlash risk today ([license notes](notes/license_models_legal_mechanics.md)). If, after 90 days of a licensed, released, documented free tier, fewer than roughly 25 organizations are identifiably using it, the honest conclusion is that the component is a portfolio asset and a lead magnet, not a product, and the paid tier should wait.

**Handsontable case study.** Handsontable ran an MIT core plus a paid Pro edition from 2011 and found that "the ratio of commercial to free users is about 1 to 25," which did not cover costs; on 2019-03-06 (v7.0.0) it merged Pro into the main package and replaced MIT with a custom "free for non-commercial and evaluation" license, keeping code on GitHub and letting objectors stay on 6.2.2 ([Handsontable blog](https://handsontable.com/blog/?p=1263)). The HN thread drew 112 points and 73 comments about forks, bait-and-switch, and advice to use AGPL dual licensing instead ([HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=19488642)), yet npm downloads went from **~143k/month in March 2019 to ~1.49M in the last 30 days**, roughly 10x ([npm API](https://api.npmjs.org/downloads/point/last-month/handsontable)). Enforcement is an offline `licenseKey`: a plain-string `non-commercial-and-evaluation` key silences everything, a missing or invalid commercial key covers the grid with an uncloseable modal, and an expired key only warns ([license key docs](https://handsontable.com/docs/javascript-data-grid/license-key/)). Since roughly 2024 the company moved to quote-only enterprise pricing with a 2-seat minimum, 45-day trial, and a 90-day commercial evaluation window written into the 2026 non-commercial license ([pricing](https://handsontable.com/pricing)). The lesson: the switch did not kill adoption because the product already had seven years of MIT distribution and a 22k-star brand; the free tier that was "too generous" was still what built the funnel.

**AG Grid case study.** Niall Crosby started solo in 2015 because other grids "couldn't pin columns" and "couldn't do row grouping"; the launch post was read 6,000 times in 24 hours, Enterprise launched in 2016 at £250 per developer, "banks from all over the world started coming forward," one customer paid "10 times more" for a stronger license, revenue reached roughly £1,000 a day, and he had two employees by the end of year one ([JavaScript Jabber transcript](https://www.ag-grid.com/blog/javascript-jabber-podcast/)). The gated features are the "big-table" ones hobbyists never need: server-side row model, pivot and aggregation, Excel export, master/detail, integrated charts, tool panels ([Community vs Enterprise](https://www.ag-grid.com/javascript-data-grid/community-vs-enterprise/)). Enterprise ships on public npm, runs without a key for local testing, and shows a watermark plus console error in production until a key is set ([license install](https://www.ag-grid.com/javascript-data-grid/license-install/)); Enterprise downloads run at **~55–60% of Community downloads** (7.76M vs 15.1M per month), which shows how little friction the public-npm-plus-watermark model adds ([npm API](https://api.npmjs.org/downloads/point/last-month/ag-grid-enterprise)). The company hit 1,000 enterprise customers in 2017, reached ~60 people self-funded, and combined with Bryntum under Adelis in November 2025 ([AG Grid about](https://www.ag-grid.com/about.md); [Bryntum](https://bryntum.com/blog/bryntum-and-ag-grid-join-forces/)). The lesson: an MIT core removed adoption friction, inbound enterprise demand set the price, and per-developer licensing let procurement say yes.

## License models: open core wins on every dimension that matters to a solo embedder

Scores are 0–10, higher is better for Byron's situation (solo maintainer, client-side React component that customers embed in their own commercial SaaS, zero adoption today).

| Model | Adoption / SEO | Enterprise legal friction | Enforcement effort | Piracy risk | Solo-maintainer burden | Total | Reasoning |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Open core: MIT core + proprietary Pro (AG Grid, MUI X, FullCalendar) | 9 | 9 | 6 | 5 | 6 | **35** | MIT core clears allowlists untouched; Pro EULA is the form procurement already approves. Key is offline, "designed to be public," leaks tolerated ([MUI X licensing](https://mui.com/x/introduction/licensing/)). Cost: two packages and a registry seam. |
| Free + paid services/examples (React Flow) | 10 | 10 | 2 | 3 | 3 | 28 | Nothing to gate; works for a 5-person team with 38k stars whose users cannot ship a competitor's attribution ([xyflow](https://xyflow.com/blog/asking-for-money-for-open-source)). Support hours sold are a solo founder's scarcest asset. |
| Dual GPL/AGPL + commercial (lightGallery, fullPage.js, DHTMLX) | 5 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 | 27 | Made solo money for site widgets, but Google's policy says AGPL "MUST NOT be used" ([Google](https://opensource.google/documentation/reference/using/agpl-policy)), and GPL on a frontend component distributed to browsers taints the host's whole UI, so SaaS buyers avoid rather than buy. lightGallery's author concedes he cannot tell who uses it commercially ([deepakness](https://deepakness.com/raw/js-library-business-model/)). |
| Fully proprietary, free with watermark (amCharts, ZingChart, SciChart) | 3 | 5 | 6 | 5 | 7 | 26 | Simple single package, but no OSS funnel; custom license routes every user to legal; adoption 1–2 orders of magnitude below MIT-core peers ([chart vendors](notes/chart_editor_vendors.md)). |
| Source-available non-commercial (Handsontable, PolyForm NC) | 4 | 3 | 5 | 5 | 6 | 23 | Worked for Handsontable after seven MIT years; for a 0-star package it means "consult the OSPO before any use" ([OSPO Alliance](https://ospo-alliance.org/faq/licences/05-approved-licenses/)) with no brand to justify it. |
| Fair source FSL/BSL (Sentry, HashiCorp) | 5 | 4 | 3 | 2 | 7 | 21 | Sentry states FSL "is not suited to businesses based on ... library embedding" ([Sentry](https://blog.sentry.io/introducing-the-functional-source-license-freedom-without-free-riding/)); embedding in a non-competing product is a Permitted Purpose, so there is nothing to sell. |

**Pick: open core.** The free tier is MIT (not PolyForm or FSL) because every incumbent that grew on public npm uses MIT for the core and none ships a paid tier under FSL/BSL/PolyForm. The paid tier is a proprietary EULA, not AGPL, because the paying persona embeds the component in a commercial SaaS front end. The deliberate concession is enforcement: like MUI and AG Grid, accept that keys leak and rely on the EULA, a watermark, and key text that names the licensee.

## Feature split: free single-table workspace, paid multi-source, tracing, scale and embedding

The free tier must solve a real problem alone, or the model fails the open-core test that buyers apply ("the vendor decides what stays free" is a scored risk in procurement evaluations) ([GovStack](https://govstack-global.atlassian.net/wiki/spaces/DEMO/pages/96043009/Component+Library+Evaluation)). MUI's rule is to gate only features with "no great MIT alternatives" ([MUI X licensing](https://v6.mui.com/x/introduction/licensing/)). The split below mirrors AG Grid: commodity charts and linked filtering free; multi-source, scale, explainability and embedding paid.

Packaging: `exploreda` (MIT) keeps today's name and registers only free chart definitions; `exploreda-pro` re-exports the core and registers the rest, validating the key on `register`; `exploreda-license` holds the key check. The chart registry becomes the enforcement seam, which also fixes the current defect that the root entry registers all 20 charts at import and that `composition` and `scatter-matrix` have no `./charts/*` entry ([readiness audit](notes/exploreda_readiness_and_feature_split.md)). Upgrade is an import change, as with MUI's `-pro` supersets.

## Price sheet and the arithmetic to $2k, $10k and $50k a month

Anchors: MUI X Pro $299/dev/yr and Premium $599 ([MUI](https://mui.com/pricing/)); AG Grid $999/dev perpetual with one year of updates ([AG Grid](https://www.ag-grid.com/license-pricing/)); FullCalendar Premium from $480/yr with 50% early-renewal discount ([FullCalendar](https://fullcalendar.io/pricing)); fullPage.js $477 lifetime business and $990/yr OEM/SaaS ([alvarotrigo.com](https://alvarotrigo.com/fullPage/pricing/)); amCharts OEM at ~4.3x its SaaS seat price ([amCharts store](https://www.amcharts.com/online-store/)); Kanaries Graphic Walker $1,900/yr per domain ([Kanaries](https://kanaries.net/graphic-walker)); SciChart's founder picked the midpoint of competitor prices and found the middle decoy tier sold best ([Mixergy](https://mixergy.com/interviews/scichart-with-andrew-burnett-thompson/)).

| Tier | Unit | Annual | Perpetual | Scope |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Community | n/a | $0 | MIT | Single-table workspace, core charts, calculations, saved JSON, CSV. Production use allowed. |

Hold Pro at $299 as a launch price and reserve a move to $399–$499 after the first LTS line and VPAT exist; MUI left Pro at $180 for six years and had to raise it 66% in one step ([MUI price changes](https://v8.mui.com/blog/2026-mui-x-price-changes/)), so publish a cap on annual increases (MUI 7%, AG Grid 5% plus CPI) from day one.

**Model to the three targets.** Assume an average first-year order of **$450** (1.5 seats at $299), a **70% renewal rate** (no component vendor publishes churn; this is an assumption), and that 10% of customers add the $990 OEM SKU, giving a blended average annual contract value of about **$550**. Then $2k/month ($24k/yr) needs ~44 paying organizations at steady state, $10k/month needs ~220, and $50k/month needs ~1,100. With Handsontable's 1:25 commercial-to-free ratio ([Handsontable blog](https://handsontable.com/blog/?p=1263)) those targets imply roughly 1,100, 5,500 and 27,000 organizations using the free tier. Using the 0.1% visitor-to-purchase rate the GTM note derives from Pintura's launch data and HN signup rates (an assumption, not a measurement), reaching the ~4 new orders a month that sustain $2k requires on the order of 4,000 unique docs/demo visitors a month; $10k requires ~20,000; $50k requires ~100,000. For scale: `@kanaries/graphic-walker` sits at 5.6k npm downloads per week and sells per-domain embedding licenses ([npm API](https://api.npmjs.org/downloads/point/last-week/@kanaries/graphic-walker)), so $2k/month is a plausible year-2 outcome for a React EDA component, $10k/month is the fullPage.js/Pintura band reached after three or more years, and $50k/month is either a 5–7 year solo outcome (Pintura, $40–50k/month) or a team outcome (SciChart hired from year one and reached ~$2M/yr by 2020) ([Plugged In](https://matthews-newsletter-35a04a.beehiiv.com/p/rik-schennink-grew-pintura-50kmonth-focusing); [Mixergy](https://mixergy.com/interviews/scichart-with-andrew-burnett-thompson/)).

## Buyers and channels: vertical-SaaS product teams, found through docs, a free sibling tool and search

Three segments are worth naming. **Vertical-SaaS product teams** who need a "explore your data" screen inside their app are the highest-value buyers and the ones that need the OEM/SaaS SKU; fullPage.js and Bryntum both carve this out as a separate, pricier license, and Bryntum prices it by end-customer count ([Bryntum OEM](https://bryntum.com/oem-pricing/)). **Internal-tools and data-platform teams** pay only once a component becomes shared infrastructure; Sacra's read on Refine is that "popularity shows up long before willingness to pay" and the main competitor is building in-house ([Sacra](https://sacra.com/chat/h/e5a36e72-aa77-4b99-afbd-2cac0f2dfe7a/)). **Scientific and engineering teams** (SciChart's F1 teams, NASA, pharma) buy on performance with real data volumes and hands-on support ([Mixergy](https://mixergy.com/interviews/scichart-with-andrew-burnett-thompson/)), which explorEDA cannot yet claim because no capacity envelope beyond a 10,000-row walkthrough has been measured. Developers drive these purchases: 66% of professional developers report influence over technology purchases and over 80% investigate on their own ([Stack Overflow](https://stackoverflow.co/advertising/resources/seat-at-the-table)), so the first buyers will pay by card below ~$1,500 and only multi-seat or OEM deals go to PO.

The three channels with founder evidence are, first, **docs and demo pages that rank in search**: lightGallery's author says "all the traffic comes from Google" ([deepakness](https://deepakness.com/raw/js-library-business-model/)), and Adam Wathan says "the docs are the only way people find out about our commercial products," which is also why Tailwind's revenue fell ~80% when docs traffic fell ~40% ([DevClass](https://www.devclass.com/2026/01/08/tailwind-labs-lays-off-75-percent-of-its-engineers-thanks-to-brutal-impact-of-ai/)). Second, **a free sibling that links to the paid product**: about one in three Pintura customers arrive from FilePond's README ([Plugged In](https://matthews-newsletter-35a04a.beehiiv.com/p/rik-schennink-grew-pintura-50kmonth-focusing)); the explorEDA analogue is the existing exploreda.dev viewer positioned as a free "paste a CSV, explore it" tool, plus a tiny MIT utility (the calculations parser or the DSL checker) with its own README. Third, **building the prototype in public**: SciChart's early blog posts produced emails asking price and ship date before launch ([Mixergy](https://mixergy.com/interviews/scichart-with-andrew-burnett-thompson/)), and PyGWalker's Show HN drew 712 points for exactly the Tableau-style-exploration experience explorEDA delivers in React ([HN Algolia](https://hn.algolia.com/api/v1/search?query=pygwalker&tags=story)). Channels to skip: Product Hunt produced two subscriptions from 300k impressions for Pintura, paid Google and Twitter ads changed nothing for fullPage.js, and GitHub Sponsors "didn't work at all" for React Flow ([GitNation](https://gitnation.com/contents/making-an-open-source-library-financially-sustainable)). HN interest in "embedded analytics" launches is low (most under 15 points), so lead with the end-user experience, not the category.

## What must be true before charging, ranked by how often it blocks a sale

| Rank | Gap | Why it blocks | Effort |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | No license anywhere (repo, `package.json`, npm) | Automated license scanners fail unlicensed packages before any human looks ([Black Duck](https://www.blackduck.com/blog/node-js-license-security-risks.html)); nobody may legally use the package today. | Hours |
| 2 | No commercial terms: EULA, price, seat model, trial, enforcement | Procurement has nothing to approve. | Days plus lawyer |
| 3 | npm 0.1.0 trails the demo and README by 112 changesets | Buyers evaluate by installing; the installed product differs from the demo and reads as abandonment. | Days |
| 4 | No stability promise; docs say breaking changes are acceptable; `migrateDataVersion` is a no-op | A host persisting `SavedDataStructure` needs a format guarantee; buyers score deprecation windows ([Sencha](https://www.sencha.com/blog/how-to-evaluate-ui-component-libraries-10-criteria-that-actually-matter/)). | Policy doc plus a real migration path |
| 5 | No support channel, SLA, PR CI, single maintainer, 0 stars | "Guaranteed response times" and a continuity plan are on every checklist ([Handsontable checklist](https://handsontable.com/blog/choosing-the-enterprise-javascript-ui-component-the-checklist)). | Days for CI and a written promise |

| 7 | No SECURITY.md or disclosure contact | Vendors publish audits, scanning and `security@` ([Handsontable security](https://handsontable.com/docs/react-data-grid/security/)). The positive facts (no network calls, OIDC provenance) are unpublished. | Hours |

| 9 | No docs site; root README lists 12 of 20 chart types; no API reference or StackBlitz starter | Buyers test whether they can find a working example for their hardest case quickly. | Weeks |
| 10 | SSR/Next.js undocumented; module-scope `registerAllCharts()` and CSS import | Next.js is the default React host; an unanswered "does it work in Next" stalls trials. | Days to document, more to support |
| 11 | No published data-size envelope; a 27k-row filter once took 34 s | Analytics buyers ask "how many rows?" in the first call. | Benchmark run and a published number |

## 90-day plan with kill criteria

The plan's purpose is to make the free tier legally usable and credible, instrument adoption, and collect the evidence that decides whether the paid tier launches in month four. It does not build the paid tier until week 9.

| Week | Milestone |
|---|---|
| 1 | MIT `LICENSE` and `license` field; move `@tailwindcss/postcss` to devDependencies; generate THIRD_PARTY_NOTICES in the build; delete or move `AUDIT.md`, `UI_UX_AUDIT.md`, `TODO.md`, transcripts out of the public tree; add SECURITY.md and a `security@` alias. |
| 2 | Cut the release that flushes the 112 changesets (0.2.0); add PR CI running tests and `check-types`; fix repo hygiene (root name, pnpm version pin, `engines`, `sideEffects`, `./charts/*` entries for composition and scatter-matrix). |
| 3 | Publish a stability policy: saved-data format versioned with a real migration path, deprecation window, LTS statement; publish browser matrix and the SSR/Next.js recipe (`"use client"` plus `dynamic(..., { ssr: false })`). |
| 4 | Minimal docs site: install, three StackBlitz starters (single table, Next.js, project mode), API reference generated from types, one page per chart. Fix the README chart list. |
| 5 | Benchmark: row-count sweep at 10k, 100k, 500k rows with interaction timings; publish the envelope and the 34-second trap fix. Publish bundle numbers with react and react-dom external. |
| 6 | Position exploreda.dev `/viewer` as the free "paste a CSV, explore it" tool with a persistent link to the package; write the launch post around linked filtering and row tracing (the PyGWalker experience, in React, with cross-chart linking). |
| 7 | Show HN and r/reactjs post, 8–10am Eastern, reply to every comment for two hours ([Causo playbook](https://hub.causo.ai/guides/show-hn-launch-playbook-technical-founders-2026)); submit to JavaScript Weekly and React Status as news, not sponsorship. |
| 8 | Instrument: npm weekly downloads, GitHub stars, docs uniques, issues opened by non-Byron accounts, inbound emails. Add a "Using this at work? Tell me" form on the docs. Start a buyer-interview list (target ten conversations). |
| 9 | Design the registry seam: free package registers free charts, `exploreda-pro` registers the rest and validates a key on `register`; build the offline key format and generator; draft the EULA for lawyer review. |
| 10 | Lawyer review of EULA and CLA; set up CLA Assistant; open a Paddle or Polar account; build the key-issuing webhook. |
| 11 | Publish the pricing page with Pro at $299/dev/yr, the OEM add-on, the indie free key, and a trial form, marked "available from <date>"; publish the free/paid feature table. |
| 12 | Decision week: apply the kill criteria below; if passed, ship `exploreda-pro` 1.0 with the first ten trial keys hand-issued to interview contacts; if failed, keep MIT and switch to the fallback. |

Kill criteria at day 90, measured over weeks 8–12: fewer than **25 identifiable organizations** using the free package (by inbound form, issues, or named production use), fewer than **1,000 npm downloads per week** sustained, fewer than **3 of 10 interviews** naming a gated feature (project mode, tracing, advanced charts, embedding) as something they would pay for, and zero unsolicited "can we use this commercially" or "do you offer support" emails. Any three of those four means the paid tier waits and the component stays MIT. The fallback is not to abandon the product: keep it MIT, treat it as the free sibling that advertises Byron's analytics and React work, sell fixed-scope integration engagements to the first teams that ask, and revisit the paid tier at six months with the same criteria. The evidence says the three-year free-distribution runway is the norm, not a failure, and a watermark-removal or attribution tier (React Flow style) can be added at any point without relicensing because the core stays MIT.

## Open questions only Byron can answer

Whether any explorEDA commits were made under an employment agreement with an IP-assignment clause, which decides whether relicensing is unilateral. How many hours per week he will give to support, because per-developer licensing sells support response times and a solo maintainer's time is the real constraint. Whether the goal is a side income in the $2k/month band or a full-time business, since the former fits open core at $299 and the latter needs the three-year runway and probably a hosted product or a hire. Which segment he knows personally (vertical SaaS, internal tools, science), because the first ten customers in every founder account came from the founder's own network and blog readers. Whether he is willing to narrow the product to the 10k–500k-row client-side sweet spot and say so, or whether he wants a DuckDB-WASM or server-computation story before selling to data teams. Whether he will keep the public repo as the canonical source for the Pro code (AG Grid style, visible but EULA-licensed) or keep Pro in a private repo (FullCalendar Premium style). Whether the exploreda.dev viewer should become a free hosted product in its own right, which changes the funnel and the pricing unit. And whether he already has an audience (newsletter, Twitter, blog) that shortens the runway the way Wathan's book did for Tailwind UI.

## Conclusion

The decision is less "sell or not" than "when, and what stays free." Every comparable that reached meaningful revenue grew a free artifact first and monetized the slice enterprises cannot get elsewhere; the ones that tried to gate client-side code behind registries or restrictive licenses without a prior audience either reverted (Tiptap) or sit one to two orders of magnitude below MIT-core peers in adoption. explorEDA's distinctive features, linked filtering in a finished React UI, row-level tracing, multi-source project mode, and a host-owned JSON state contract, are exactly the kind of "no great MIT alternative" features that justify a Pro tier, and no free tool surveyed ships them together. What is missing is not product; it is the license, the release, the promises and the users.

Choosing open core now costs nothing and removes the one risk that is irreversible: relicensing after adoption. The 90-day plan converts an unlicensed 0-star repo into a legally usable, measurable free tier and ends with a numeric go/no-go. If the numbers come in, Pro at $299 per developer per year with an OEM add-on is a price procurement already understands and a model one person can run with no license server. If they do not, the right move is still to keep shipping the MIT core, because in this market the free tier is the business development budget.
