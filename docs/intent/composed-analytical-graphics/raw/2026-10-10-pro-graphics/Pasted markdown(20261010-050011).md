You are a data-visualization researcher. Find published graphics that a small "composition" editor should be able to rebuild. For each graphic, capture what it looks like, explain how it breaks into simple building blocks, and say where its data can be found.



\## Background: what the editor is



explorEDA is a React library for exploratory data analysis. Its new composition mode lets an author build one polished, report-ready graphic on a fixed-size artboard, starting from a blank page. The author does not pick a finished chart type. They assemble the graphic from a few parts:



\- **\*\*Artboard:\*\*** a fixed pixel size and a paper color. The output is a PNG for a report or slide.

\- **\*\*Text elements:\*\*** title, subtitle, notes and source lines, and free text. Text can include computed values, such as "{Total} messages".

\- **\*\*Named scales:\*\*** shared definitions that map a field to position, color, size, or length. By default, repeated panels share one scale so they can be compared directly.

\- **\*\*Chart unit (template):\*\*** one frame and the marks drawn inside it. A mark definition draws one glyph per bin or record from that unit's data, as a rectangle or circle encoded by color, size, or height.

\- **\*\*Repeat rule:\*\*** splits the rows by a field and draws the unit once per value, arranged as rows, columns, or a grid. This produces small multiples.

\- **\*\*Calculations:\*\*** count, sum, average, min, or max. Each one covers either a single repeat or the whole graphic, and either follows or ignores the active filters. Example: a per-row total beside each label, or a fixed overall average line.

\- **\*\*Guides:\*\*** reference rules at a fixed or calculated position.

\- **\*\*Annotations:\*\*** callouts anchored to the page, to a frame, or to a data mark that they follow when the data changes.

\- **\*\*Instance overrides:\*\*** visual exceptions for one repeat, such as a nudge, accent color, fade, or bold label.



**\*\*What exists today.\*\*** Every unit is a one-dimensional strip. Its x position is a date bin (day, week, month, or year) or a category. Each bin gets one rectangle or circle, encoded by color, size, or bar height. Units repeat as rows, columns, or a grid. The model reference is FlowingData's 2026 strip graphic of email volume per correspondent per month: one row per person, one colored square per month, a total beside each name, and a few callouts.



**\*\*What is planned, in order:\*\***



1\. **\*\*Ordered paths:\*\*** lines that connect records in a declared order, such as a connected scatterplot ordered by year. This needs a numeric x and y frame.

2\. **\*\*Interval bands:\*\*** filled areas between a lower and an upper bound, such as forecast fan charts and confidence bands.

3\. **\*\*Paired distribution summaries:\*\*** quartiles and medians calculated per group and compared between two cohorts, such as before/after dumbbells with an interquartile range (IQR).

4\. **\*\*Normalized stacking:\*\*** 100% stacked areas or bars with traceable denominators.

5\. **\*\*Compound frames:\*\*** two coordinated frames in one repeated unit, such as a detail view plus an overview inset.



**\*\*Already collected; do not return these:\*\*** the FlowingData email-correspondent strips (2026), the NYT/FlowingData EV price-versus-range comparison, the NYT "Driving Shifts Into Reverse" connected scatterplot, the Bank of England February 2025 inflation fan chart, FlowingData's "How We Spent Our Time in 2020 Versus 2019", FlowingData's "Causes of Death" by age, and the Washington Post unvaccinated case-rate state comparison.



\## What to find



Find **\*\*20 to 30 graphics\*\*** that are more involved than an ordinary dashboard chart but are still built from the parts above. Good candidates usually have most of these traits:



\- One repeated unit (small multiples, rows, or a grid) plus shared text, scales, and annotations, all designed as one graphic.

\- Simple marks: rectangles, circles, lines, areas, rules, and text. The interest comes from layout, repetition, calculation, and annotation, not from exotic geometry.

\- A clear editorial message carried by titles, direct labels, and a few callouts.

\- A data shape someone could plausibly have as a table, from about 50 to 50,000 rows.



Aim for variety. Cover at least eight of these families, with no more than four examples from any one family:



\- Strip or heat-strip rows, calendar heatmaps, and timeline grids

\- Small-multiple line or area charts with a shared scale and highlighted series

\- Dot plots, dumbbells, and range or interval bars, repeated by group

\- Slope charts and bump charts

\- Waffle, unit, and pictogram-free icon-grid charts

\- Tile-grid maps, where each state or country is a square cell holding a mini chart

\- Ridgelines, horizon charts, and stacked strips

\- Sparkline tables, including Tufte-style tables of tiny lines with labels and values

\- Fan or band charts and uncertainty ribbons

\- 100% stacked bars or areas and Marimekko-style share charts

\- Annotated single-frame editorial charts whose difficulty is layering and annotation, not repetition

\- Du Bois-style or other historically notable composed graphics



**\*\*Exclude:\*\*** geographic maps that need real projections or shapes; network and node-link diagrams; Sankey and alluvial diagrams; treemaps and sunbursts; 3D graphics; anything that depends on animation, scrollytelling, or interaction to make sense; a single bar or line chart a standard dashboard tool would produce; and illustration-heavy infographics where data is a small part.



Search widely, including the following sources. Prefer examples whose data you can obtain.



\- Editorial graphics: FlowingData, NYT Upshot and Graphics, Financial Times (including the FT Visual Vocabulary), The Economist Graphic Detail, Reuters Graphics, Bloomberg, the Washington Post, The Pudding, Axios, and Our World in Data.

\- Practitioner sources: the Datawrapper blog, Storytelling with Data, Information is Beautiful Awards entries and shortlists, and Junk Charts.

\- Sources with available data, which are strong for this task: TidyTuesday, Makeover Monday, the W\.E.B. Du Bois Visualization Challenge, Tableau Public Viz of the Day, the Observable Plot and D3 galleries, the Vega-Lite example gallery, and R Graph Gallery or Python Graph Gallery recreations of notable charts.

\- Statistics agencies and central banks, such as the Bank of England, the Fed, the ONS, the BLS, Eurostat, and the IMF, which publish chart data beside their graphics.



\## What to return for each graphic



Use exactly this structure.



\### N. Title of the graphic



\- **\*\*Creator and publisher:\*\*** author(s), outlet, and publication date.

\- **\*\*Page URL:\*\*** the page where the graphic appears.

\- **\*\*Visual:\*\*** a direct URL to the image file. If you can save files, also download the image as \`images/NN-short-slug.png\` (or \`.jpg\`) and give that path. If the graphic only renders in a browser, take a screenshot of the graphic alone. A description alone is not enough; every entry needs an image the reader can open.

\- **\*\*What it shows:\*\*** two or three sentences on the message and how the eye reads it.

\- **\*\*Why it fits:\*\*** one or two sentences on why this is a good composition target: the step beyond a dashboard chart, and the part that makes it worth rebuilding.

\- **\*\*Composition breakdown:\*\*** how an author would build it from the parts listed above. Cover all of these:

&#x20; \- Artboard: approximate size and aspect ratio.

&#x20; \- Text elements: title, subtitle, labels, notes, and any computed values in text.

&#x20; \- Scales: each field to position, color, size, or length; and whether repeats share a scale or each have their own.

&#x20; \- Unit template: the frame and the marks drawn in one repeat, with shape, encoding, and aggregation.

&#x20; \- Repeat rule: the field it repeats by, the arrangement, the order, and how many units there are. Write "none" for a single frame.

&#x20; \- Calculations: each derived value, with its population (one repeat or the whole graphic) and whether it follows filters.

&#x20; \- Guides, annotations, and highlighted repeats: what each one attaches to.

\- **\*\*Capability tier:\*\*** choose one.

&#x20; \- **\*\*Today:\*\*** buildable with 1D strips of rectangles or circles plus repeats, text, guides, and annotations.

&#x20; \- **\*\*Planned M2–M6:\*\*** name the milestone(s) it needs: paths, bands, paired summaries, normalized stacks, or compound frames.

&#x20; \- **\*\*New capability:\*\*** name the missing part plainly, such as "a numeric y scale for points", "direct text labels per mark", "a legend element", "per-mark sorting by value", "diverging bars around a baseline", or "a tile-grid arrangement driven by row and column fields".

\- **\*\*Minimum data table:\*\*** the columns and grain the composition needs, such as \`state, year, rate (one row per state-year, about 2,500 rows)\`. Note any pre-aggregation.

\- **\*\*Source data:\*\*** choose one, and give the evidence.

&#x20; \- **\*\*Available:\*\*** URL(s) of the data file or API, format, license or terms if stated, and the cleaning or reshaping steps needed to match the minimum table. Confirm the link works and the columns match. Do not assume.

&#x20; \- **\*\*Obtainable:\*\*** where the underlying public data lives, such as the agency and table name, and the steps to rebuild the needed table. State what is uncertain.

&#x20; \- **\*\*Synthetic:\*\*** the real data is unavailable or impractical. Give a short generation recipe that keeps the graphic's character: columns, row count, distributions, the trends or outliers that must appear, and a random seed. Label it synthetic.

\- **\*\*Difficulty:\*\*** low, medium, or high, with one sentence on the hardest part.



\## How to work



\- Open every page you cite. Do not invent URLs, dates, authors, or column names. If you can't verify something, say so in the entry.

\- Prefer original publications to reposts. When a recreation (Observable, R Graph Gallery, Vega-Lite) has clean data, cite both the original and the recreation.

\- Judge fit honestly. A graphic that needs a new capability is still useful if the capability is small and reusable. Say so rather than forcing it into the "Today" tier.

\- Quote at most one short phrase from any source. Summarize in your own words.
