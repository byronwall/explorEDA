# Independent performance and correctness review

Reviewed 2026-10-20 America/Indianapolis (2026-10-21 UTC). Research only: no application, upstream test, or benchmark was executed. Arithmetic summaries below were calculated from four already-published CSV files. The paper is v1; the benchmark harness is pinned to `72b82e1bad3e96786e63bb10877b4d5cd48cc2f5` (2025-06-25); current Mosaic implementation inspection is pinned to `9ca1d860e30ed148d0b228b92ebf30f66cb9e2ce` (2026-10-15). ## 1. What the measurements establish

Paper §7 measures materialized-view construction and selection-update queries for five templates. Flights adds a distance histogram to the three-view configuration. NYC taxis and Gaia update several rasters and histograms. Airlines exercises average and standard deviation through confidence intervals for 26 airline groups. UK property shows a large density plot but **only its regression fit updates in the benchmark**. Its result therefore does not measure rebuilding or repainting the whole density plot. Interval sweeps use widths of 10%, 20%, and 30% of axis ranges; the airline slider visits its positions. [P, §7]

The six row-count conditions are 10,000 through one billion in powers of ten. Flights and property use sampling with replacement plus noise in numerical columns to reach 100 million and one billion. The motivating datasets have different sizes: 44.8 million flights, approximately 1.3 billion taxi trips, over 1.8 billion Gaia stars, and 29.5 million UK properties. Those source examples should not be confused with a billion independent, observed flights or property transactions. [P, §§2, 7; B, `bin/bench.js:13`]

The reported environment is Node.js 20.11.1 on a 2021 16-inch MacBook Pro, M1 Pro, 16 GB RAM, macOS 14.5, using DuckDB 1.0 and DuckDB-WASM 1.29.0 with default settings. This is a Node-based experiment, including the WASM condition. It is neither an explorEDA measurement nor a browser event-to-paint experiment. The paper's four-gigabyte and single-thread remarks describe its WASM configuration and period, not an independently verified claim about every current browser/backend. [P, §7]

The native/local materialization results are reported as at most one second through 100 million rows, rising to one to two minutes at one billion. WASM construction is reported below one second through ten million; all one-billion conditions and the 100-million Gaia and taxi conditions run out of memory while loading. Optimized query updates are generally 1–10 ms and below 100 ms in the evaluated conditions. These are published workload results, not service-level guarantees. Figure 12 presents medians and interquartile ranges, with a 60-fps reference line; that line does not establish rendered frame rate or tail-latency performance. [P, §7, Fig. 12]

VegaFusion and VegaPlus comparisons reuse earlier published flight-histogram results from Yang et al. The paper does not report a fresh, identical-machine, all-template comparison of those systems. Its attribution of differences to SQL/runtime architecture is an interpretation, not a separately isolated causal experiment. [P, §7]

## 2. Timing, loading, and cache qualifications

The inspected harness creates one connector and loads its table once per run. It records loading as its own stage. `bin/bench.js` sets `parallel=true` and `view=false`; `src/run.js` therefore loads a table rather than using a view over the input file. Tasks belonging to one stage, active view, and update ID are submitted together, and their promises settle before the next group. Raw records time `db.query`; the analysis reduces a group to **latest completion minus earliest start**. Consequently the summarized CSV value is a query-group completion envelope, not the mean duration of its individual SQL statements. [B, `src/benchmark.js:18–108,143–153`; `src/analyze.js:3–20`; `src/run.js:9–22`; `bin/bench.js:17–18`]

The plotting script separates `create` and `update` stages and omits `load`. The paper's use of cold start therefore refers to on-demand materialization after the base data is available; it must not be rewritten as full cold application start, network transfer, parsing, and indexing in under a second. No explicit OS-cache flush, separate warmup protocol, or repeated-process design was found in the inspected harness. Describe this limitation, without asserting that every run is either wholly cold or wholly warm. [B, `web/results/results.js:6–11,53–69`; `src/benchmark.js:24–47`]

Descriptive recalculation of the archived CSV medians supplies useful sanity checks:

| Archived condition | Load (ms; one record) | Create median (ms) | Update median (ms) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Flights, native optimized, 100M | 3383.144 | 589.226 | 1.624 |
| Flights, native standard, 100M | 3419.788 | — | 306.699 |
| Property, native optimized, 10k | 177.168 | 5.549 | 0.764 |

These are summaries of existing published records, not newly generated timings. Optimized and standard update counts differ by one in each pair, so avoid presenting them as exact paired per-interaction speedups. The property records support the paper's observation that optimization can add overhead at small sizes. [B, the four CSV files under `results/bench/` listed in Sources]

## 3. Appendix A: prefetching and sorting

The neuron example has 384 channels, approximately 2,500 samples/second for 71 minutes, about 10.7 million samples per channel, and over 4.1 billion rows. Sorting by time aligns block statistics and physical access with panning. The experiment compares direct requests, tiled requests, and adjacent-tile prefetch, for time-sorted and channel-ordered data. Figure 14 distinguishes median and mean and shows skewed tails. These results support workload-specific locality and prefetch hypotheses. [P, Appendix A]

The actual timing includes constructing/stitching a numeric grid after the query result, but not browser painting. Crucially, the harness records the current interaction's duration and **then awaits the adjacent prefetch promise before issuing the next interaction**. Thus the next request receives full prefetch lead time, while that wait is outside the reported current-interaction latency. This is a favorable latency experiment, not a measurement of uninterrupted pointer-driven throughput or free background work. [B, `src/tile-pan.js:38–58,72–84,103–134`]

There is a minor paper/harness counting difference: the paper describes ten skips each followed by 100 steps; source defaults generate ten blocks of 100 requests, or 1,000 total, with each block's first request serving as the jump. Do not claim 1,010 executed requests from the paper wording alone. Generalized automatic tile creation/stitching/prefetch remains future work in the paper; this example uses a specialized client. [P, Appendix A.2–A.3; B, `src/tile-pan.js:8–33`]

## 4. Appendix B: cardinality is not a memory budget

Each materialized table corresponds to an interactor/client pair. The paper bounds its rows by the product of interactive resolution and client bins, with sparse occupancy often below that product. The §5.2 illustration uses 600 pixels × 24 hours = 14,400 rows, while Table 5's flight benchmark uses 540 × 24 = 12,960. Those are different resolutions, not contradictory arithmetic. Flights' 540 values also appear in the pinned sparsity script. [P, §5.2, Appendix B; B, `bin/sparsity.js:72–75`]

Paper-reported representative maxima are 440 for property regression; 6,240 for airlines; 12,960–16,200 for flight histogram pairs; 2,519,638,416 for Gaia raster-to-raster; and 2,134,440,000 for taxi map-to-map. These maxima explain why a method efficient for histograms can be inappropriate for dense cross-raster arrays. They are **row counts**, not observed bytes; dimensions, aggregate columns, database overhead, table multiplicity, and resident query results still matter. [P, Appendix B, Table 5, Fig. 15]

Two provenance discrepancies need explicit qualification if exact maxima are reproduced. The pinned `bin/sparsity.js` uses 242 airline slider values, giving 6,292 with 26 groups, whereas paper Table 5 uses 240 and 6,240. The source also uses a Gaia color/magnitude dimension of 278 where Table 5 uses 282. Further, Table 5's magnitude-histogram to color/magnitude entry says 24 bins and 5,640 maximum rows, unlike its other large color/magnitude entries. These were not reconciled empirically. Report paper and source values under their own provenance; do not silently correct or merge them. [P, Table 5; B, `bin/sparsity.js:63–83`]

The paper suggests a storage-bounded LRU for materialized tables as future work. The current implementation's default 256 MiB LRU applies to **query results**, not all materialized tables. `PreAggregator.clear()` removes local entries but expressly leaves tables in the database. `dropSchema()` removes those tables and is documented for base-table changes. SQL construction hashes do not themselves establish dataset-version validity. Any later adapter needs explicit generation invalidation and a separately measured table-storage policy. [P, §8, Appendix B; U, `preagg/PreAggregator.ts:126–148,204–225`; `util/cache.ts:23–57`; `QueryManager.ts`]

## 5. Correctness gates before any optimization claim

**Pixel bins.** Current interval preaggregation bins both source coordinates and endpoints, then applies inclusive `BETWEEN`. Under identity scale and unit floor bins, raw `[10,20]` excludes 20.9 while the binned predicate admits it. This is a static counterexample, not an executed upstream regression. Binned display semantics can be appropriate, but exact row membership, lasso trace, and CSV require a raw-predicate recheck or authoritative row-ID set. A raster is not evidence that every source row was individually selected. [U, `preagg/PreAggregator.ts:266–289,301–325`]

**Union.** The paper expressly excludes top-level UNION resolution from its preaggregation derivation. The inspected current request path filters by inactive clauses before applying the active predicate and exposes no explicit union guard. That is an unresolved static concern, not a reproduced defect. A later integration should disable preaggregation for unions until optimized/unoptimized set parity is demonstrated; disjunction within a single supported point clause is a different case. [P, §5.1; U, `preagg/PreAggregator.ts:163–235`; `Coordinator.ts`]

**Sufficient statistics.** A combined average must weight group means by valid-value counts, or use total sum divided by valid count. Current `avgExpr` implements the weighted-mean form. Variance uses centered residual sum, residual sum of squares, valid count, and sample/population correction. Covariance and correlation use pairwise valid counts and the necessary division-by-count terms. The current source contains those normalization factors; the visually suspicious equations in paper Figure 9 should not be copied as executable formulas. Empty populations, singleton sample variance, missing values, high-offset data, and zero-variance correlation require explicit oracle cases and numerical tolerances. Count and sum must distinguish no valid values from a real zero. [U, `preagg/sufficient-statistics.ts:175–178,229–324`]

These gates favor a small internal selection contract over a speculative backend migration. Performance evaluation should follow semantic parity: measure actual explorEDA datasets and interaction sequences, separate loading/building/warm updates/painting/export, record median and tail latency plus memory, and include sparse/dense, first interaction, repeated interaction, source switching, and data replacement. The paper supplies mechanisms and workload evidence; it does not establish a bottleneck or an adoption threshold for this repository.
